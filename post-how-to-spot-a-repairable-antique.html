Buying Advice

How to Spot a Repairable Antique

July 28, 2026 · 6 min read · by the Heritage & Grain bench

Five checks that tell you whether that flea-market find is worth restoring — or worth walking away from.

Most people judge an old piece by its surface, which is exactly the wrong place to start. Finish is the cheapest thing to put right; structure is the expensive part. Before anything else, put both hands on the frame and push gently sideways. Movement in the joints is normal on a hundred-year-old chair. Movement combined with a cracked rail is a different conversation.

Second, look at the back and underside. Original saw marks, hand-cut dovetails and unevenly coloured timber all suggest an honest piece. Perfectly uniform machine dovetails and stapled backs usually mean a twentieth-century reproduction, which can still be lovely but should be priced accordingly.

Third, check for woodworm exit holes and press a fingernail into the timber near them. Firm wood with old, dust-free holes is historic damage and rarely a problem. Soft, crumbling wood with fresh frass is active and needs treating before the piece comes anywhere near your home.

Fourth, count the missing parts. A missing drawer runner is twenty minutes of work. A missing carved cresting rail is a day of hand carving and a matching colour job. Losses on show faces cost far more than losses you will never see.

Finally, be honest about what you want. Conservation keeps the history and the patina. Restoration makes it look new. Both are valid, they cost different amounts, and knowing which one you want before you buy will save you a great deal of money.
